### Health indicators

An indicator is a measurement that reflects a given situation. For our purposes, it is a quantitative metric that is used to monitor performance (:measure results against targets) and assess accountability.  

Selection of health indicator could be narrowly or broadly defined. Examples:
- Narrowly defined i.e:  reflect the **coverage of an intervention** and associated  **outcomes and impacts.**
- Broadly defined i.e: show **health service access across the continuum of care** for a particular condition. 

The **WHO Monitoring, Evaluation and Review Framework** organizes health indicators according to four indicator domains: (1) Inputs and processes, (2) Outputs, (3) Outcome , (4) Impact indicators.

### Tracer & Composite indicators 

 **Tracer indicators** are **specific indicators** that stand in for a broader health topic. Tracer indicators are straightforward to understand and report, although they may not be representative of the processes involving the broader topic. 
**Composite indicators** combine multiple indicators, sometimes across different health topics, into an index, thus generating an **overall picture of the health topic**.

 Using an indicator with a **standardized definition** can facilitate **benchmarking**, as other populations may have comparable data about the indicator.  WHO Global Reference List contains a standard list of 100 core health indicators, plus indicators for the health-related Sustainable Development Goals. 

### Measuring health indicators

Health indicators are generally measured as absolute counts or relative terms (such as proportions or rates). Indicators measured in relative terms are constructed as a numerator and denominator. For a proportion, the numerator is a subset of the denominator. For example, the percentage of births attended by skilled health personnel is the number of births attended by skilled health personnel (numerator), divided by the total number of live births (denominator). 
### Dimensions of inequality 

 Dimensions of inequality are the criteria used to define population subgroups that serve as the basis of comparison for health inequality analyses.
Certain inequality dimensions have common applicability and data availability across most settings and topics. As well, **context-specific dimensions of inequality are numerous** and varied, including caste or tribe, criminal status, indigenous status, language spoken at home etc... 

### Considerations when selecting dimensions of inequality 

- Certain dimensions may not be applicable (Disaggregating the use of maternal health services by the sex of the individual or sex of the child, for instance, does not make sense.).
- Selection of dimensions of inequality should involve consultation with relevant stakeholders. 
	- Single dimensions or multiple dimensions more adapted?
- **Intersectionality** – defined as the coexistence of multiple characteristics and identities that **together compound disadvantage** due to the interactions among them – may warrant consideration
	- Double (applying two dimensions of inequality) or multiple disaggregation is a starting point of exploring **intersectionality through health inequality monitoring**.
