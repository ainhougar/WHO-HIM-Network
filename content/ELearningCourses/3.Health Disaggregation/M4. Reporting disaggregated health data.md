
### Best practices & advantages of disaggregated data

The interpretation of **disaggregated data tends to be intuitive**, therefore they are appropriate for most audiences. In some cases, the main conclusions from the data can be adequately supported using disaggregated data, without the need for other technical content such as summary measures of health inequality.

- Disaggregated data should be reported alongside other pertinent information (overall average, sample sizes, uncertainty measures) to situate the results.  Explaining why monitoring was undertaken, in other words, providing context, is important. 
- **Describing patterns** in disaggregated data can be a **compelling way** to assess and report disaggregated health data for dimensions of inequality that are ordered, such as age, economic status or education

### Example of patterns 
Example of inequality pattern are represented by the graphs below. 
- **Complete coverage**: green line
- **Pattern of marginal exclusion**, purple line. The poorest quintile has much lower coverage than the four richer quintiles. 
- **Queuing or linear pattern**, blue line. There are increases in coverage across each of the quintiles. 
	  - Intervention: A combination approach, **with differentiated targeting across the population** subgroups is warranted. 

- **Mass deprivation pattern**,red line, most of the population – all but the richest in quintile 5 – has low levels of coverage.
	 -Intervention: **population-level response** may be required to reach all (or most) of the population
