
### Simple VS Complex summary measures 

#### Simple measures
Simple measures make pairwise comparisons between two subgroups. They are especially relevant for dimensions of inequality that are binary. 
- Comparisons can be made between any two subgroups that are of special interest. In some cases, it may be appropriate to calculate pairwise measures multiple times for different combinations of subgroups.  
- The two most basic simple measures of inequality are difference and ratio. 
#### Complex measures

 Two major types of complex measures: ordered dimensions of inequality VS non-ordered dimensions of inequality.  
 - Ordered dimensions of inequality have an inherent positioning and can be ranked. 
 - Non-ordered dimensions of inequality are based on criteria that cannot be logically ranked, such as subnational regions, languages spoken, race or ethnicity.  

Ordered and non-ordered complex summary measures can be further described as **weighted** or **unweighted**. 
- Weighted measures take into **account the population size of each subgroup**
- Unweighted measures treat each subgroup as equally sized. 

While complex measures may be weighted or unweighted, simple measures are always unweighted. 

Some complex measures of inequality require the use of a reference point, which serves as a benchmark or point of comparison.

Reference points that are subgroups: subgroup at the extreme, subgroup with special significance, best-performing subgroup. 

Other reference points: overall average of the health indicator, health indicator target.

### Absolute VS Relative inequality measures 

Simple and complex summary measures of health inequality can be broadly divided into **absolute inequality measures** and **relative inequality measures**. Absolute and relative measures of inequality **provide complementary perspectives** to assess a situation of inequality. Therefore, they should be both calculated and taken into account when interpreting findings.
